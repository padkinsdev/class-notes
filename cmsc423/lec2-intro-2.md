# Some Basics

## Central dogma of biology
- The central dogma of biology states that DNA is transcribed via RNA polymerase into RNA, then ribosomes translate RNA into proteins, and finally proteins form networks and pathways which perform a vast set of cellular functions
- The AT/GC pairings are called "canonical pairings", though rarely non-canonical pairings will occur
- The 5-prime end of DNA is the phosphate group, and the 3-prime end is the hydroxyl group. Reading a strand of DNA involves reading from the 5-prime to 3-prime end
- In humans, most DNA is non-coding, though the distribution is not uniform
    - In bacteria, the vast majority of DNA is coding (~98%)
    - Non-coding DNA may or may not produce transcribed RNA
- Sometimes RNA that is transcribed from DNA is not actually translated into proteins
- In prokaryotes, genes are typically contiguous DNA segments, whereas in eukaryotes genes can have complex structure
    - Introns are removed from pre-mRNA during splicing
    - Exons appear in the mature RNA transcript
- There is a great deal of redundancy built into the Transcription/translation process, making it very robust

## RNA
- RNA is similar to DNA, with a less regular structure
- RNA is generally a single-stranded molecule
- Secondary and tertiary structure can affect function
- RNA can be a transcript for proteins, but also performs important functions itself
- Transcription can produce any form of RNA, broadly speaking, but almost always transcribes into mRNA
- While many different nucleotide triplets code for the same amino acids, the particular triplet impacts the speed of translation

## Proteins
- The shape (conformation) of a protein is intimately tied to its function. Thus, protein shape is conserved through evolution, often even through sequence mutations
- Proteins can bind with other proteins to form protein complexes

## Closing notes
- The vast majority of species' genomes are unsequenced, and they cannot be cultivated in a lab
