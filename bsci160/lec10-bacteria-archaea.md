# Bacteria and Archaea

## Bacteria
- Bacteria were discovered by Antoine van Leeuwenhoek in 1683 by using a homemade microscope
- In the 1880s Robert Koch developed the germ theory of disease by working with anthrax in cattle
- Bacteria have a cell wall and a cell membrane but not a nuclear membrane (being single-celled with no cell membrane makes them prokaryotes)
- Bacteria take the form (physically) of cocci (e.g. streptococci, diplococci, etc.), bacilli (e.g. salmonella typhi, clostridium botulinum, etc.), vibrios, spirilla, spirochaetes, etc.

### Archaea vs. bacteria
- Archaea were discovered in the 1970s by Carl Woese
- While archaea look like bacteria, they are in fact very different:
    - Different proteins in cell walls
    - Different cell membrane components
    - Different cellular machinery for transcription and translation
    - Not known to be pathogenic
-Archaea are actually closely related to eukaryotes due to genetic similarity, similar cellular machinery, use of histones, etc.

## Human microbiome
- The human microbiome consists of bacteria, fungi, viruses, archaea, etc.
    - 38 trillion bacterial cells vs. 30 trillion human cells in the human body means that we are technically more non-human than human, so to speak

## Archaea
- Archaea can inhabit extreme environments
    - Methanogens: Use H2 to reduce carbon dioxide to methane
    - Halophiles thrive in water 10x as salty as the ocean
    - Thermophiles live in environments up to 105 degrees Celsius
    - Acidophiles live in environments below pH 1

## Prokaryotes
- Prokaryotes reproduce via binary fission, and can get genetic material from the environment or from other individuals via horizontal gene transfer
    - Horizontal gene transfer can occur via transformation or conjugation
- Lateral gene transfer creates complexity in phylogenetic determinations due to the mixing of genetic material between species

## Eukaryotes
- All eukaryotes have a common archaean ancestor

# Endosymbiosis
- A single endosymbiotic event in which an archaean engulfed a proteobacteria led to the mitochondria as a cellular structure
- A single endosymbiotic event in which an archaean engulfed a cyanobacteria led to the chloroplast as a cellular structure
- Evidence for endosymbiosis includes:
    - The fact that mitochondria and chloroplasts retain their own circular genomes
    - DNA sequences of both structures are most similar to ancestral bacteria
    - Both structures have extra membranes that came from being engulfed
- Evolution of mitochondria (from a single ancestor) allowed cells to perform aerobic respiration, thus enabling the evolution of multicellular life 600 million years ago
- The uptake of mitochondria occurred before the uptake of the chloroplast, and all eukaryotes have mitochondria but not all have chloroplasts

## Multicellularity
- Most groups of eukaryota did not become multicellular
    - Of 119 major groupes of eukaryotes, 83 contain only single-celled organisms
    - Unicellular eukaryotes often function as parasites or eat other microorganisms/organic particles
    - There is a range between unicellularity and multicellularity in eukaryotes
- Some organisms switch between being unicellular and multicellular via clonal multicellularity or aggregative multicellularity
- Growing larger as an alternative to multicelluraity is limited by the issue of surface area to volume
    - As a cell grows, its volume increases faster than its surface area. Thus, the surface area of the cell membrane cannot grow quickly enough to absorb all of the necessary nutrients for the cell's functioning
- Multicellularity allows for specialization, decreased risk from predation, longer lifespan, and potentially better efficiency/adaptation, but it also increases energetic demand, slows reproduction, and demands coordination. As such, multicellularity is a high-risk, high-reward endeavor
- Properties of multicellular organisms:
    - Adhesion molecules to adhere cells to one another
    - Most individual cells retain reproductive and other function
    - Every cell is in contact with the external environment
    - Intracellular communication is essential
- Master regulatory genes guide gene expression necessary to make a particular organ
