# Animals

## Characteristics of animals
- Animals are ingestive heterotrophs with no cell membranes
- Animals are multicellular, digest food internally, engage in movement during at least one part of their life cycle, and most have nervous systems
- Animals are a monophyletic group with a common protist ancestor
    - Precisely, animals evolved multicellularity indepedently of fungi
- Animals can roughly e divided into monoblastic/diploblastic and triploblastic/bilaterian
    - This grouping is founded on the appearance of the animals, though nervous system typology can also be used as a grouping

### Body plans
- Body plan: General structure or arrangement of organ systems, as well as integrated functioning of body parts
    - Features: Type of symmetry/number of tissue layers, segmentation, external appendages, and nervous system

#### Symmetry
- Symmetry/number of tissue layers come from early division of the zygote
    - Zygote first divides into a blastula, or a hollow ball of cells
    - As development continues, infolding of the blastula creates distinct tissue layers. This is called gastrulation
        - 3 layers can form: endoderm, mesoderm, and ectoderm
- Animals are either symmetrical or asymmetrical
    - Symmetry can be either
        - Radial: Body parts arranged around a central axis
        - Bilateral: Can be divided into mirror image halves on only one plane
- True tissue is a tightly connected layer of cells that work together for a specific function
    - The number of tissue layers is connected to symmetry:
        - 0 tissue layers means assymetrical
        - 2 tissue layers means radial symmetry
        - 3 tissue layers means bilateral symmetry
- Cephalization is correlated with bilateral symmetry, and entails development of the head along with movement of sensory organs to the front
    - This allows the brain to interpret and respond to signals
- Segmentation facilitates specialization of body regions, as well as allowing the animal to alter body shape and control movements precisely
    - Mammals, like other animals, exhibit segmentation!
- Appendages enhance an animal's ability to move around, feed, smell/taste, and reproduce
- Nervous systems are useful for sensation and communication
    - Nerve nets are diffuse nervous systems in early animals
    - In contrast, bilaterians have well-coordinated central nervous systems, allowing for coordinated movement
    - Nerve nets are considered a form of convergent evolution

## Examples
- Sea sponges:
    - Can move
    - Are filter feeders, creating a current to pull through and catch organic matter
    - Have no nervous system
    - Have 0 distinct tissue layers (just a few cell types) and thus are asymmetric
- Cnidarians (e.g. jellyfish):
    - Can move with simple muscles
    - Some capture prey, some are filter feeders
    - Have a stomach but only one opening
    - Have a simple nerve net and some have simple eyes
    - Have 2 tissue layers and are thus radially symmetric
- Bilaterians:
    - Have a "through gut" with 2 openings. Food goes through a food tube from the mouth to the anus
    - The two types of bilaterians are protostomes and deuterostomes, divided by what occurs in the blastopore
    - Protostomes develop a mouth first during gastrulation
    - Deuterostomes develop a mouth second during gastrulation
    - In protostomes, nerve chords are ventral (on the belly/underside), whereas in deuterostomes they are dorsal (on the back)
    - Lophotrochozoans like mollusks and annelid worms have segmentation and some have advanced nervous systems
    - Ecdysozoans like nematodes, crabs, and insects molt and shed an exoskeleton (made of chitin)
    - Insects have over 1 million described species with modifiable exoskeletons, wings, small size, and fast reproduction
        - Of all living organisms, insects make up a majority. As such, we are in **the age of insects**
